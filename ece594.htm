Linking Distributed Classrooms using Internet2

Principal Investigator: Pat Hanrahan, CS/EE (hanrahan@cs.stanford.edu, 3-8530)
Associated Stanford faculty and staff: Armando Fox, Terry Winograd, Maggie Saunders (SLL),  Milton Chen, John Gerth, Charlie Orgish  
Associated UCSB faculty: Ed Chang

Motivation

Traditionally, distance learning technologies have been used to deliver lectures to students at remote sites. An interesting alternative, however, is to support linking distributed classrooms and laboratories, where faculty and students located at different sites are brought together at the same time. Linking classrooms provides a mechanism to bring together geographically dispersed faculty and students with shared interests. Developing technology-rich "learning spaces"  is a major goal of the Stanford Learning Laboratory.  Internet2 technology is needed to deliver the guaranteed bandwidth and latency that is critical for linking these spaces.

We would like a system that (eventually) would allow the following scenario to take place in our future classroom. The two linked classrooms are outfitted with multiple digital cameras with computerized pan and tilt stages. The digital, compressed outputs of these cameras are connected directly to a control and routing computer connected to a high speed network. As in today's SITN classrooms, the main camera captures the lecturer; the view of this camera is controlled by a computer and automatically tracks the lecturer as he moves around the classroom. Each site also contains several audience cameras. The main audience camera captures a wide angle shot of those in attendance and other audience cameras capture other people and objects of interest. The remote feed of the audience is projected in the back of the room so from the point of view of the lecturer in the front of the room, the remote audience appears to extend behind the local audience. People and operators in attendance at the remote sites may teleoperate the cameras at the other site.  The front of the classroom consist of a large display surface organized as a large virtual blackboard. Images of the people speaking at the remote site are inset into the virtual blackboard and are visible to all.  The virtual blackboards are used to project PowerPoint slides and other visual material as well as serving as a distributed virtual drawing surface. Finally, the lecturer is able to run simulations and other demonstrations, and these may be controlled from either site.

Experiment

To support scenarios like the above we have built an "Interactive Room" in the basement of the Gates Computer Science Building (see picture above). The room consists of 5 large display surfaces. The virtual blackboard, which we can an Interactive Mural, is constructed from 3 SmartBoards and measures 18' by 4 1/2'. The images from three projectors are back-projected onto the SmartBoards. We also have developed a back-projected Interactive Table. The room itself is controlled by a small cluster of approximately 12 PCs. Our goal is to use the Interactive Room as a distributed classroom and to do this we will need to acquire and deploy new networking, audio and video infrastructure. 

The software and hardware infrastructure created will be used to co-teach a course during the upcoming Winter Quarter, 2000. The Stanford version of the course is CS448: Special Topics in Computer Graphics: Experiments in Digital Television and will be taught by Pat Hanrahan; the UCSB version of the course is ECE594: Special Topics in Electrical and Computer Engineering: Internet Computing and Digital TV. The course will be open to upper level undergraduate students and graduate students who are interested in advanced television technologies. Some of the course projects will consist of enhancing the basic infrastructure with a goal of moving towards a complete implementation of the scenario described above.

The course will run from 2:30 to 4pm on Mon and Wed and the current plan is to co-teach the Mon classes. The network will be used to bi-directionally transmit to two compressed video feeds, as well as other control information. Our current plan is to use DV cameras. Each DV stream requires 25 megabits per second of bandwidth. Thus, if two videos streams are sent from each site, we will need slightly more than 100 megabits of total bandwidth (50 in each direction).

In contrast to the Stanford classroom,  the UCSB class will be taught in their standard department conference room, with a limited number of cameras and a single projector and monitor. The Stanford site has substantially more A/V capabilities than the UCSB site. In the latter stages of the course, we will assess and compare technologies used at both sites.

Budget

 The $20K would be used for the following purposes:

  • Networking: Equipment needs to be acquired to directly connect computers in Interactive Room directly to the Gates network backbone.  Our plan is to acquire a Cisco 2924 (24port 10/100, w/2-100FL ports, $1700) to support the room cluster and have the fiber go directly from it into our Cisco 5500 switch/router. 
  • Audio and Video equipment: Four camera systems (Sony DCR-PC1 DV camera ($1300) + Adaptic DV card ($200)) and one microphone array and multi-input audio capture card ($1000)
  • Personnel: 1 graduate student RA for 1 qtr to install the equipment and develop various pieces of glue software. Milton Chen, who will also TA the course, will be available to help in this regard.
