\section{Related Work}

Color histograms have been widely used to index images in large image 
databases. They are easy to compute and have proven useful for
content-based image retrieval. However, since color 
histograms do not contain spatial information of colors,
images of the similar colors but different distributions 
can be mistreated as similar ones.
Besides, color histograms can be very sensitive
to minor image alteration such as cropping,
adding borders and framing.
We thus deem that color histograms may not be 
the appropriate feature set for image copy detection.

Several methods have been proposed to remedy
the shortcomings of color histograms.
Pass and Zabih [1,2] developed the {\it color coherence vector} 
(CCV) method,
which partitions each color into a coherent and an incoherent
portion. CCV is still easy to compute (about ten times slower than color 
histograms) and has been shown to produce better results than color histograms
in similarity search. However, CCV can neither cope with
adding borders or framing. This is because adding borders or frames
increases the number of pixels in certain coherent color buckets
significantly and thus the magnitude distance (which is used as the
indicator of similarity between images in CCV) can be large between the
modified and the original image. As a result, CCV tends to deem the
modified image not similar to the original one. Hsu et al. [3] selects a set
of dominant colors from a image and partitions the image into 
rectangular regions, each contains one color. The similarity of two
images is then determined by the degree of overlap between regions of the
same color. Smith and Chang [4] employ histogram back-projection [5] to
partition the image into regions. They allow one region to have multiple 
different colors and the colors of each region are represented by a
binary color set. The binary color sets and their location information
combined is the feature vector for an image. This approach can support
querying by the spatial locations of the color regions. However, there is
no performance report on the feature vectors for Hsu's
and Smith's algorithms. Since they both involve some computationally
expensive algorithms to partition the image into regions, they will not be
able to generate the feature vectors in a time comparable to color
histograms. Besides, the feature vectors of both approaches depend on the
pixel positions. So it may not tolerate large appearance changes such as
transitions or rotations. Huang et al. [6,7] tries to encode the spatial
correlation of color-bin pairs by the "color correlogram". It has been
shown to be more effective in similarity search than color histograms and
CCV as it includes the local spatial correlation between colors into the
feature vector. It has also been shown to be robust to large image
appearance changes. But while computing the feature vector, for each pixel
of the image, this approach needs to survey the colors of a good amount of
the surrounding pixels. So the computational complexity is much higher
than that of the color histograms, which only need to survey the color of
the pixel itself.

    Texture and shape can also serve as feature vector. The application of
texture-based feature vector is largely limited to the high resolution,
textural images. As for the shape-based feature vector, it often requires
the segmentation of the image into color homogeneous regions, which is a
very hard problem itself and requires intensive computation. 

    Our approach tries to find an efficient way to incorporate the spatial 
information of the colors into the feature of images. By introducing the
variances of colors into the color histograms, we compose the
Variance-Augmented Color Histogram (VACH). The use of variances can be
traced back to the seven moments method proposed by Hu[8], who uses the
variances of colors as one of the seven moments to do shape analysis and
at the same time circumvents the hard problem of segmenting the image into
homogeneous color regions. Since color variances are indicators about the
global distribution of colors, they give a good idea about how the colors
are distributed in the image. At the same time, they do not depend much
on the exact position of the pixels. This makes them robust to the large
appearance change of images. VACH can be computed in a single pass over
the image and the computing time is comparable to that of color 
histograms. VACH is especially effective in finding the similarity between 
the image and the image with borders or frames because it weights color
regions of high variance less than that with low variance. VACH can also
serve as a general-purposed feature vector in the image retrieval system.
