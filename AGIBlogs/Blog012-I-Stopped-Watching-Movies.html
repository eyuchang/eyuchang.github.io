Dispatch No. 12 · AI, Hollywood & the Future of Movies

Only Popcorn Is Left Behind

Digital technology has been reshaping media for decades. Over the past two years, generative AI has intensified the pressure on knowledge work—and now the pain around Hollywood is accelerating again. My own iPhone habit finally made the shift impossible for me to treat as abstract.
Edward Y. Chang•Stanford InfoLab•October 21, 2026•12 min read

AI displacement is not a new conversation. Long before the current generative-AI wave, digital technology had already unsettled newspapers, the press, publishing, music, and the movie industry. But over the past two years, the discussion has intensified sharply as generative AI moved into programming, mathematics, research, and other knowledge work.

Now the pain around Hollywood is accelerating again—and being reported with new urgency. Two of my daughters live in Los Angeles, so this is not abstract to me. This week I listened to KQED's Forum ask, "Can California Save Hollywood?" The discussion focused on productions leaving Southern California, competing tax incentives, soundstage infrastructure, and the long-term future of the industry.[1] Two days earlier, Oren Hadar made a complementary argument: Los Angeles cannot save Hollywood if young actors, musicians, writers, and other creatives can no longer afford the years of uncertainty required to become good at their craft.[2]

Restaurants may seem like a different industry altogether, but for generations Hollywood and restaurants were joined by one familiar social bundle: dinner and a movie. Today both are being unbundled by digital convenience, delivery, mobile screens, and increasingly generative media.

Those reports made the problem timely. What finally forced an acknowledgment, however, was noticing my own behavior. The disruption was no longer something I was forecasting. My own habits had already changed.

I have not gone to a movie theater in years. I do not even watch the free feature films offered on long business-class flights. I have never subscribed to Netflix. I do not pay monthly fees for short-movie apps. Yet lately I have been watching, on average, about two movies a day.

They are not conventional two-hour movies. They are short, often around thirty minutes, and I watch them on my iPhone. I have started to joke that I am becoming "addicted" to them. The number of available stories seems almost endless.

What surprises me most is not only the quantity. It is how quickly the production quality has crossed a practical threshold. On a small screen, human faces, animals, landscapes, interiors, motion, and cinematic transitions now look convincing to me perhaps 90 percent of the time. The remaining flaws are usually not important enough to interrupt the story. Voices can be dubbed or regenerated into different languages. Characters and settings can be re-versioned across national contexts, voices, appearances, and narrative styles.

Hollywood is asking how to bring movie production back to Los Angeles. I am beginning to wonder whether the audience is quietly changing the definition of a movie at the same time.

Hollywood's production crisis is real

There is no reason to minimize what is happening in Los Angeles. KQED's October 21 discussion describes productions migrating to other states and countries as rivals offer stronger incentives and production infrastructure.[1] The Los Angeles Times reported in August that second-quarter 2026 shoot days for feature films and television were down about 20 percent and 30 percent respectively from already weak 2025 levels, and that Los Angeles had lost roughly 57,000 film and sound-recording jobs over four years.[3]

Hadar's essay adds an important human layer. His argument is not simply that California needs more tax credits. It is that creative ecosystems need slack: the cheap apartment, the waitressing job, the odd gig, and the years in which an artist can fail repeatedly before finding a voice or a career. He recalls paying $725 a month for an East Hollywood apartment in 2002 and argues that the disappearance of such economic slack is pushing young creatives out before they have time to mature.[2]

I find that argument persuasive. But it addresses mostly the supply side of Hollywood: who can afford to live in Los Angeles, where productions are filmed, and whether the local ecosystem can retain talent.

My iPhone habit points to the demand side: what audiences are increasingly willing to call entertainment.

The movie itself may be changing

Short drama is no longer a marginal format. Sensor Tower reports that short-drama apps exceeded 850 million worldwide downloads in the first quarter of 2026, up 140 percent year over year. Global in-app purchase revenue was roughly $750 million for the quarter. Average daily time spent in short-drama apps had reached about 25 minutes by April, compared with roughly 35 minutes for conventional OTT streaming.[4]

Those numbers do not mean that all short dramas are AI-generated. They are not. It is important not to collapse two different trends into one.

Revolution 1

The format changes

Stories become shorter, mobile-first, serialized, highly abundant, and designed for frequent consumption rather than a two-hour appointment.

Revolution 2

The production stack changes

Generative AI increasingly lowers the cost of creating scenes, actors, voices, localization, visual effects, and eventually entire narrative productions.

The interesting possibility is that these two revolutions are now beginning to converge.

A studio is becoming software

That second transition is no longer hypothetical. In an October 8 PBS NewsHour segment distributed by KQED, a writer using the EyeTell platform produced a period-drama episode in about a week without real actors, real locations, sets, or horses. The system integrates generative-video tools so that an individual creator can perform work that once required a much larger production organization.[5]

KQED had already devoted a June Forum episode to AI's expanding use in editing, animation, visual effects, and filmmaking, and to the tension between new creative tools and an art form historically built around human storytellers and production communities.[6]

This matters because the traditional movie bundled many expensive constraints together:

Large production organizationPotentially a small team or even one creator coordinating AI tools
Fixed actors and physical setsSynthetic or hybrid actors, scenes, locations, and effects

Sensor Tower's report itself points to increasingly diversified monetization, including advertising, rather than a single subscription model.[4] That aspect resonates strongly with my own behavior. I have never been a Netflix subscriber, and I am not paying a monthly fee for the short films I watch. This is not a story about one streaming subscription replacing another. For me, the more fundamental change is that the friction to begin watching has nearly disappeared.

Only the popcorn is left behind?

The traditional movie was rarely just a movie. It was an evening architecture: leave home, meet someone, eat at a restaurant, stand in line, sit in a theater, and perhaps buy popcorn. The economic value was distributed across several industries because the experience required physical movement.

Restaurants are certainly not disappearing. The National Restaurant Association projected U.S. restaurant and foodservice sales of about $1.55 trillion in 2026 and employment of roughly 15.8 million people.[7] But the way people use restaurants is changing. The Association reported in 2025 that nearly three-quarters of restaurant traffic was already off-premises—takeout, delivery, and drive-thru—making eating restaurant food at home an ordinary part of daily life.[8]

This is not an argument that AI is "killing restaurants." Delivery, mobile ordering, cost, convenience, and changing lifestyles are all involved. But AI is entering this layer too. DoorDash's 2026 industry survey reported that 22 percent of consumers had used AI to choose a restaurant, while restaurants increasingly use AI for discovery, reservations, and customer service.[9]

The more interesting point is that the old bundle is being unbundled. The movie comes to the phone. The restaurant meal comes to the door. Discovery becomes algorithmic. Dubbing and localization become generative. A night that once required leaving home can increasingly be reconstructed on the sofa.

Perhaps only the popcorn is left behind: the ritual survives, while almost everything around it—the theater, the restaurant visit, the subscription, even parts of the production itself—is being recomputed.

The two-hour movie was never a law of nature

We often treat cultural formats as if they were intrinsic to the art. They usually are not. They are compromises among technology, economics, distribution, and human habit.

The feature film was shaped by theaters, projection, ticket economics, schedules, physical distribution, and an audience willing to devote an evening to a single work. Television inherited half-hour and hour-long blocks from broadcast schedules. Streaming removed the schedule but largely preserved the long-form unit.

The phone removes another assumption: that a story must command a large uninterrupted block of time.

The old movie was scarce in production and abundant in audience attention. The emerging movie may become abundant in production and scarce in attention.

If so, thirty minutes is not an inferior two-hour movie. It may be a different equilibrium.

Localization may become generation

Dubbing used to be a downstream operation. A finished film existed first; translators, voice actors, subtitlers, and distributors adapted it for another market.

Generative systems blur that boundary. Language, voice, visual identity, background, setting, and even characters can increasingly become variables rather than fixed properties of one master copy.

That does not mean culture becomes interchangeable. Quite the opposite. Bad localization can erase culture. Synthetic stereotypes can make it worse. Rights, consent, provenance, and compensation become more important, not less.

But technically, the direction is striking: the same narrative structure can increasingly be re-expressed for different audiences without reproducing the entire physical production.

When that becomes normal, a movie may no longer be a single immutable artifact. It may become a family of generated versions.

Does this mean Hollywood no longer matters?

No. That conclusion would be too easy.

AI can make production cheaper. It cannot make every story worth watching. It can generate a convincing face without creating a memorable character. It can reproduce visual grammar without necessarily understanding why a scene matters. And if synthetic content becomes nearly infinite, abundance itself makes another resource scarce: taste.

This is where Hadar's argument about Los Angeles may become even more important rather than obsolete. A creative city is not valuable only because it contains soundstages. It is valuable because people collide there: actors, writers, musicians, editors, designers, engineers, directors, immigrants, eccentrics, failures, and future successes. The "slack" that allows such people to experiment is precisely what a world flooded with mechanically generated content may need most.[2]

If production capacity becomes abundant, human imagination, judgment, cultural authenticity, and the ability to recognize a story worth telling may become more valuable.

Saving Hollywood may mean saving something different

KQED asks whether California can bring film and television production back.[1] That remains an urgent economic question. Tens of thousands of livelihoods depend on it.

But I would broaden the question.

Saving Hollywood should not mean preserving every production workflow exactly as it existed in 2019. It should mean preserving and renewing the creative ecosystem that made Hollywood valuable while accepting that the medium itself may change.

Preserve

The creative ecosystem

Affordable paths for young creators, writers, actors, musicians, editors, designers, and craftspeople to develop judgment and voice.

Reinvent

The production model

Shorter formats, generative production, global localization, creator-scale studios, and new economic models beyond theater tickets and monthly subscriptions.

California may succeed in restoring tax competitiveness and still miss the larger transformation if the product being brought back is optimized for an audience habit that is disappearing.

And AI companies may make the opposite mistake: believing that because they can generate moving images, they have reproduced Hollywood. They have not. A rendering engine is not a culture.

The paradox on my iPhone

My own behavior is only one person's behavior. It proves nothing by itself.

But I find the paradox difficult to ignore.

I stopped going to movies. I do not watch the free movies available to me on airplanes. I never subscribed to Netflix. I do not pay a monthly short-film subscription. Yet I am consuming more narrative video than I have in years.

The screen became smaller. The movies became shorter. The supply became nearly infinite. The production increasingly looks synthetic. And instead of making me watch less, all of that made me watch more.

Meanwhile, the dinner that once accompanied the movie can increasingly arrive at the same sofa. The old ritual does not vanish all at once; it is disassembled into pieces and delivered separately.

Los Angeles is debating how to bring Hollywood home.

But what if "home" is no longer a studio lot, a theater, or even a streaming service?

What if the next movie is twenty or thirty minutes long, generated by a tiny team, re-versioned into many languages and settings, discovered algorithmically, and watched on the phone while sitting on a sofa?

Then the challenge is larger than saving production.

We have to save the people who know what stories are worth telling, while being willing to let the movie itself become something new.

Maybe the theater changes. Maybe the restaurant visit changes. Maybe the actors, sets, dubbing, subscriptions, and even the runtime change.

At least the popcorn survives.
