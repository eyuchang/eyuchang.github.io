Statement of Research Interests
Edward Chang

Advancement in network and compression technology and decreases in hardware costs have led to a rapid growth in multimedia applications in recent years. How to use the available system resources effectively to retrieve, deliver, and receive voluminous media data (i.e., videos, audios, and images) presents interesting and challenging problems. My research interests focus primarily on how to help users access media data fast and at minimum cost.


The primary focus of my thesis was on managing continuous media data at both the media server and media client sides. At the server side, I propose schemes to minimize initial delay to retrieve and deliver media clips to the clients while maintaining high throughput. At the client side, I propose a memory-disk integrated cache (MEDIC) to cushion against variability in the data delivery rate and against differences in delivery and consumption rates. I implemented a smart-VCR based on MEDIC, which allows the viewers to pause, catch up, and replay during a live TV broadcast. In addition to my thesis work, I recently led a research project aimed at designing and implementing a Replicated IMage dEtector (RIME) for the World-Wide Web, and I worked with HP and NEC laboratories on classifying documents using machine learning techniques.


In the media server work, I show that contrary to conventional wisdom, which holds that maximizing disk utilization benefits the system, slowing down the disk may actually lead to better throughput since it reduces the memory requirement [7,9,15]. Based on this observation, I propose intelligent data placement and disk scheduling policies [8,10,15] that give the disk arm the flexibility to service a newly arrived or a fast-scan request immediately and that thereby minimize the initial delay. I show that these techniques can be applied to manage parallel disks efficiently [4]. In the media client work, I propose MEDIC [1], which writes the arrived media data to the client's local disk when the memory is about to overflow and reads the data into main memory when the decoder's buffer is about to underflow. My study shows that with less than eight megabytes of main memory, MEDIC can support the $19.2$ Mbps Digital-TV (DTV) broadcast rate effectively, i.e., the display does not suffer from ``hiccups.'' This low main memory requirement reduces the cost to build a smart-VCR that supports interactive operations, allowing viewers to view TV programs at their own pace. Without the assistance of MEDIC at the media client side, these interactive operations either cannot be implemented (in the broadcast case) or may prolong playback time and hence significantly decrease server throughput (in the point-to-point transmission case). Some local companies (e.g., Intel and Apple) have expressed strong interest in using this technology in their DTV infrastructures.


As mentioned above, besides my thesis work, I led the research and implementation of a Replicated IMage dEtector (RIME) project for the World-Wide Web [2,9]. RIME uses a novel clustering/hashing combined approach that first clusters similar images on adjacent disk cylinders and then builds indexes to access the clusters made in this way. Searching for the replicas of an image often takes just one IO to look up the location of the cluster containing similar objects and one sequential file IO to read in this cluster. This approach effectively avoids the curse of dimensionality that the traditional tree-like indexing structures suffer by trading off some recall percentage.


Recent advancements in image-based rendering and display technology have made an array of digital-TV (DTV) applications, such as video panoramas and interactive DTV, feasible. To support these advanced features, a future media client (perhaps a combination of today's TV set and desktop) may connect to a point-to-point network while receiving broadcast signals simultaneously. Managing the resources of a media client effectively to receive both textual and continuous data from more than one source at the same time is a challenging task. In addition, the increasing demand of providing content for other clients (e.g., providing a home movie to be viewed by relatives) may elevate the role of a media client to a media server. These seemingly inevitable trends of consumer electronics will significantly affect the design of a future media client and is thus one focus of my future research.


Specifically, my research can continue in the following directions: Regarding management of the continuous media data in a point-to-point network, I plan to further explore the idea of client-assisted server load-balancing. The server may, for instance, deliver more data to the clients during the off hours to alleviate the server's workload during the peak hours. Regarding management of the continuous media data in a broadcast network, I plan to integrate error concealment algorithms into MEDIC to deal with the DTV signal disturbance problem. I have already taken the first step to devise a scheme to disperse a continuous channel error into random errors and have developed a simple and efficient image reconstruction scheme to rebuild the damaged images [6]. However, building robust error concealment schemes for live DTV broadcast is more complicated. First, the error must be contained so as not to propagate to the inter-coded frames that depend on the damaged frames. Second, the error concealment scheme must be efficient so that the frames can be displayed near real-time.


In addition to the media client/server work, I plan to continue working on the clustering/hashing algorithm as an alternative to the traditional tree-like structures in order to conduct similarity search in high-dimensional spaces. Although I believe that our clustering approach can discover what we call the ``nature clusters'' of most datasets better than schemes like k-mean and vector quantization, more experiments must be performed to reach conclusive statements. In addition, reducing the pre-processing time of our clustering algorithm and dealing with incremental clustering remain major research challenges in this area. Lastly, I have been working on applying data mining techniques (e.g., SOM, decision trees, etc.) to classify documents with HP and NEC research laboratories [2,3]. We have had many promising leads, and it is highly probable that I will be able to collaborate with these laboratories after I start my academic career.


In the past three years, I have worked on four different research projects---media server, digital TV, image databases, and document classification---and have published in each of these fields. Through these research projects I have demonstrated that I can familiarize myself with a new discipline in a short time, identify root causes of challenging problems, and devise simple and novel approaches to solve the problems. Since Computer Science is a rapidly changing field, I believe that being able to identify interesting problems promptly and to develop efficient methods for solving the problems is critical for anyone who wants to remain on the forefront of the research community. I believe that my diversified work and research experience as well as my educational background in three fields---Industrial Engineering, Computer Science, and Electrical Engineering---equip me with the ability to see problems in more than one dimension and to conduct interdisciplinary research.


I believe that being a successful researcher requires a combination of talent, drive and the desire to question conventional wisdom. Leadership in the research community requires in addition a measure of idealism and a moral compass. I believe I possess all of these qualities.
